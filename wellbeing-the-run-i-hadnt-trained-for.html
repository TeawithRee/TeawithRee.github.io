The Run I Hadn't Trained For

I used to think showing up was most of the job. If I turned up willing, I assumed the rest would sort itself out.

On Sunday I ran five kilometres on a treadmill, fast, forty minutes, with no build-up behind it, while my Garmin recorded the challenge.

My shins did not.

By the next morning they had their own account of the run. A dull ache down the front of both legs that turned sharp on the stairs, the tax a body collects when it is asked to sprint before it has been asked to walk. A few slow days followed and the humbling experience of writing about preparation from a chair.

Here is the argument I keep having with myself and I want to be honest about which side is winning.

Spontaneity is good. I mean that plainly, not as a caveat before I take it back. Some of the best things I do begin with no plan at all, a walk that turns into an hour, a page that turns into an essay. But spontaneity answered with action and no planning underneath it, is where the trouble starts. Showing up and assuming you can simply take on whatever is in front of you, a hard five kilometres, a full day, a big commitment, is not the same as being ready for it. The run made that difference physical rather than theoretical.

Planning is the part I actually want to defend here, because it is the part that gets skipped in favour of enthusiasm. My legs had not done the slower, duller work that lets a person go fast without paying for it afterwards, the gradual mileage, the build that spontaneity is happy to skip past. Feeling ready and being ready are not the same claim. Most days the gap between them costs nothing. On the wrong day it costs a fortnight and the fortnight is the planning tax you did not pay in advance.

I am not giving up spontaneity. I still think the instinct to simply begin is one of the better instincts I have. What changes is the question that comes before the impulse turns into action. Not whether I feel like it, but whether the actual groundwork exists yet to support it. Enthusiasm is not preparation. It only feels like preparation until the bill arrives.

The run was worth it, shins and all. It did not need to go well to teach me something and what it taught me was plain enough to write down without dressing it up. Show up, yes. But show up with the work already done underneath it, or be honest that you are choosing the risk, not avoiding it.

Spontaneity decides to begin. Planning decides whether you can afford to.
