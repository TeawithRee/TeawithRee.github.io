Rainforest After Rain

A single mature canopy tree moves a startling amount of water into the air every day, the kind of thing entire forests have been measured doing again and again. Rain stops and the leaves are still holding it, every one of them beaded with water that hasn't decided yet whether to fall.

The air smells different, cooler, sharper, like something has just finished exhaling.

That smell is work, not rest. A single large tree can lift over a thousand litres of water into the sky in a day, pulling it up through its roots and letting it go through its leaves.

Multiply that by a forest and you get a system that doesn't wait for rain to arrive. It makes its own, breathing water into the air until the sky has enough to give some back.

I think about that a lot when I feel like I'm waiting for something to change before I can start moving. Waiting for the right mood, the right rest, the right conditions.

But a forest never waits for its next storm. It builds it, from the inside, out of what it's already doing.

Renewal, the real kind, isn't a thing that happens to you. It's active.

It's the forest making its own weather instead of standing under a bare sky hoping for a cloud to pass over.

There's a word for that smell after rain, petrichor, but naming it doesn't explain why it feels like permission. Permission to stop holding your breath, to let something in you loosen the way the ground does after weeks of dry heat.

What would you have to start generating yourself, instead of waiting for the right conditions to hand it to you.
